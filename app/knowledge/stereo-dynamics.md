# Stereo image and dynamics

## Narrow or mono-sounding mix
tags: stereo, width, narrow, mono, panning, stereo width low, small, image

A stereo width near zero means the left and right channels are almost identical. Pan supporting elements (doubled guitars hard left and right, percussion, backing vocals, pads), keep kick, snare, bass and lead vocal in the centre, and use stereo reverbs and delays to create space. Real double-tracking sounds wider and more stable than artificial wideners. Width comes from contrast: if everything is wide, nothing feels wide.

## Overly wide mix and mono compatibility
tags: too wide, wide, phase, correlation, mono compatibility, side, stereo widener, cancellation, negative correlation

A very wide image or an L/R correlation close to zero or negative means a lot of out-of-phase content. In mono (phones, club PAs, Bluetooth speakers) parts of the mix will cancel and disappear. Check the mix in mono regularly. Cut back stereo wideners and Haas-effect delays, check the polarity of multi-mic recordings (two mics on a guitar cab, top and bottom snare), and use mid/side EQ to reduce side energy rather than widening everything.

## Keep the low end mono
tags: low end width, bass mono, stereo bass, sub stereo, vinyl, club, low end, mid side

Below roughly 120-150 Hz, stereo information makes the bass unstable: it shifts with the listener's position, cancels in mono and causes problems on vinyl and club systems. Use a bass-mono tool or a mid/side EQ high-pass on the side channel at 100-150 Hz, and make sure stereo bass synths and reverbs on low instruments aren't putting sub energy into the sides.

## Compression and punch
tags: punch, transients, compression, attack, release, dynamics, crest factor, drums, flat, lifeless

Punch comes from transients that stand out from the sustain. A low crest factor or PLR means those transients have been squashed. On drums, use slower attack times (10-30 ms) so the hit passes before the compressor clamps down, or use parallel compression to add density while keeping the dry transients. Transient shapers can restore attack on kicks and snares. On the master, prefer light gain reduction and let the mix itself provide the density.

## Too dynamic for the genre
tags: too dynamic, uneven, plr high, crest factor high, inconsistent, vocal level, quiet parts

A very high peak-to-loudness ratio on a pop or electronic track usually means a few loud peaks (drum hits, plosives) are stopping the rest from coming up. Control them first, with clip gain or a clipper on the drum bus and compression or volume automation on vocals, then the master limiter has less to do. In acoustic and orchestral music a high PLR is normal and desirable; do not chase loudness at the cost of expression.

## Sidechain and kick-bass relationship
tags: sidechain, kick, bass, ducking, low end, pumping, groove, edm, conflict

When kick and bass share the same range they mask each other and add up to peaks that the limiter has to catch. A sidechain compressor (or a volume-shaping tool) on the bass, triggered by the kick, ducks the bass for 50-150 ms on each hit. Set the depth from 2-3 dB for subtle clarity up to full pumping as an effect in EDM. Alternatively, EQ-carve: give the kick the 50-60 Hz fundamental and the bass 80-100 Hz, or the other way round.
