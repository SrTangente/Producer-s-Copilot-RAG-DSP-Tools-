# Tonal balance and EQ

## Reading a tonal-balance comparison
tags: tonal balance, eq, frequency, spectrum, reference, band energy, comparison

Comparing the share of energy per band against a reference in the same genre is the quickest way to spot a tonal problem. Differences of a few percentage points are normal; a difference of more than about 4-5 points in one band is worth investigating. Remember that most of a mix's energy sits below 250 Hz, so small changes in the low end move the percentages a lot. Fix tonal problems in the mix first (on the instruments causing them) and keep master-bus EQ moves broad and small (1-2 dB, wide Q).

## Too much sub bass
tags: sub, sub bass, 20-60 hz, boomy, rumble, 808, kick, too much low end, headroom

Excess sub (20-60 Hz) eats headroom, makes the limiter pump, and disappears or turns to mud on small speakers. Check which elements carry sub: often pads, vocals, guitars and FX carry useless rumble. High-pass everything that isn't the kick or bass (often at 80-150 Hz). Decide whether the kick or the bass owns the sub and sidechain or EQ-carve the other one. For 808s, saturation adds harmonics that let the bass translate on phones without needing more sub.

## Not enough sub or low end
tags: thin, weak low end, lacks bass, sub missing, small, low band low, kick, bass

A mix that is thin compared with the reference usually needs a stronger fundamental in the kick or bass, not a master EQ boost. Check the bass part's register (bass played too high has no sub), layer a sine or sub-oscillator one octave below, and give the kick a 50-70 Hz body. Check on headphones or a subwoofer: small monitors often make producers under-cook the low end. Low-end decisions should be made at a consistent monitoring level.

## Muddy low mids
tags: mud, muddy, low mids, 200-500 hz, boxy, congested, unclear, low_mid

The 200-500 Hz region builds up quickly because almost every instrument has energy there. Too much makes the mix cloudy and boxy. Sweep a narrow boost to find the resonances on guitars, pianos, pads and vocals and cut 2-4 dB with a moderate Q; cut on the supporting elements, not on the focal one. Dynamic EQ on the mix bus around 250-400 Hz can catch build-ups that only happen in dense sections.

## Harshness in the upper mids
tags: harsh, harshness, 2-5 khz, upper mids, high_mid, fatigue, aggressive, presence, sibilance

The ear is most sensitive between 2 and 5 kHz. Too much energy there makes a mix tiring at volume, even if it sounds exciting at first. Look for resonances on distorted guitars, synth leads and vocals; use dynamic EQ or a multiband compressor so you only cut when the peaks happen. De-ess vocals (5-9 kHz). If the mix lacks presence, a small broad lift around 3 kHz on the lead element is better than lifting the whole master.

## Dull or too bright top end
tags: dull, dark, bright, air, high frequencies, treble, 8-16 khz, spectral centroid, brightness, high band

A spectral centroid well below the reference means the mix is darker; well above means brighter. For a dull mix, try a broad high shelf (+1 to +2 dB from 8-10 kHz) on the mix bus or on the elements that should shine (hi-hats, vocals, acoustic guitars), or saturation and exciters that generate harmonics. For a mix that is too bright, check the cymbals and hats level first, then use gentle shelving or a de-esser across the bus. Make bright/dark judgments at matched loudness: louder always seems brighter.
